Mayhaw jelly

If there’s one taste that belongs to Deep East Texas, it’s this one. Mayhaws are small, tart, apple-like fruit from a thorny hawthorn that grows in wet bottomland along our creeks and rivers. In Texas they only grow north and east of the Trinity River. They bloom white in late winter and drop ripe fruit in spring, often right into the water, where folks scoop them up with sieves.

Light pink: blooming. Red: picking season.

Right here in Huntington, Wynn and Pam Havard’s Jellytree Mayhaw Farm makes it the old way: mayhaws, sugar and pectin, cooked in small batches. Pam’s advice is to freeze the berries until you’re ready. She thinks jelly from frozen mayhaws turns out better.

To make it safely, use a tested canning recipe. The National Center for Home Food Preservation publishes one for mayhaw jelly with pectin, with exact ratios and processing times. Canning is one place where you follow the tested recipe to the letter.

Cast-iron skillet cornbread

One 10-inch skillet, about 8 wedges

The bread that goes under, beside or into most of what’s on this page. Get the skillet screaming hot first. That’s where the crust comes from.

You’ll need

  • 2 tablespoons bacon grease or butter
  • 1½ cups yellow cornmeal
  • ½ cup all-purpose flour
  • 1 tablespoon baking powder
  • ½ teaspoon baking soda
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1½ cups buttermilk
  • 2 eggs
  • ¼ cup melted butter

How to

  1. Put the grease in a 10-inch cast-iron skillet and set it in the oven while it heats to 450°F.
  2. Whisk the cornmeal, flour, baking powder, baking soda and salt in a bowl.
  3. In another bowl, whisk the buttermilk, eggs and melted butter. Stir into the dry mix just until it comes together.
  4. Pull the hot skillet out, swirl the grease up the sides, and pour in the batter. It should sizzle.
  5. Bake 20 to 25 minutes, until the top is golden and a toothpick comes out clean. Turn it out onto a board and cut into wedges.

Sugar or no sugar is a family argument we’re staying out of. Add up to 2 tablespoons to the dry mix if that’s how your grandmother did it.

Cornmeal-fried catfish

Serves 4

Friday-night fish fry food. Cornmeal, not flour, and hot oil, not warm oil.

You’ll need

  • 2 pounds catfish fillets, cut into strips
  • 1 cup buttermilk
  • 1 cup fine yellow cornmeal
  • ¼ cup all-purpose flour
  • 2 teaspoons salt
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1 teaspoon paprika
  • ½ teaspoon cayenne
  • Peanut or vegetable oil for frying
  • Lemon wedges and hot sauce

How to

  1. Soak the catfish in buttermilk for 20 minutes while you heat 2 inches of oil to 350°F in a heavy pot or Dutch oven.
  2. Mix the cornmeal, flour, salt, pepper, paprika and cayenne in a shallow dish.
  3. Lift each strip from the buttermilk, let it drip, and press it into the cornmeal mix on both sides.
  4. Fry a few pieces at a time, 3 to 4 minutes, turning once, until deep golden and the fish flakes. Fish is done at 145°F inside.
  5. Drain on a rack. Salt again while hot. Serve with lemon, hot sauce and hushpuppies.

Don’t crowd the pot. Too many pieces at once drops the oil temperature and you get greasy fish.

Hushpuppies

About 2 dozen

Fry these in the same oil right after the catfish.

You’ll need

  • 1 cup yellow cornmeal
  • ½ cup all-purpose flour
  • 2 teaspoons baking powder
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • ½ cup finely chopped onion
  • 1 jalapeño, seeded and minced (optional)
  • 1 egg
  • ¾ cup buttermilk

How to

  1. Stir together the cornmeal, flour, baking powder, salt and sugar.
  2. Stir in the onion and jalapeño, then the egg and buttermilk. The batter should be thick enough to hold a shape on a spoon.
  3. With oil at 350°F, drop tablespoon-size scoops in and fry 2 to 3 minutes, turning, until brown all over.
  4. Drain and eat them hot.

Chicken and rolled dumplings

Serves 6

Flat, rolled dumplings, the kind you cut with a knife. Sunday dinner, sick-day dinner, and what shows up on the porch when somebody passes.

You’ll need

  • 1 whole chicken (about 4 pounds)
  • 1 onion, quartered
  • 2 ribs celery
  • 2 teaspoons salt, plus more to taste
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • For the dumplings: 2 cups all-purpose flour
  • ½ teaspoon baking powder
  • ½ teaspoon salt
  • 3 tablespoons cold butter or shortening
  • ¾ cup cold milk or broth

How to

  1. Cover the chicken with water in a big pot with the onion, celery, salt and pepper. Simmer about 1 hour, until the meat falls off the bone.
  2. Take out the chicken. Strain the broth and put it back on to simmer. Pull the meat off the bones in bite-size pieces.
  3. For the dumplings, mix the flour, baking powder and salt. Cut in the butter until it looks like coarse crumbs. Stir in the milk to make a stiff dough.
  4. Roll the dough thin, about ⅛ inch, on a floured counter. Cut into strips about 1 by 2 inches.
  5. Drop the strips one at a time into the simmering broth. Cover and cook 15 minutes without lifting the lid. Stir the chicken back in and cook 5 minutes more. The broth will thicken from the flour.
  6. Taste for salt and pepper. Chicken is safe at 165°F.

Hot wassail

About 12 cups

Every Saturday after Thanksgiving, downtown Nacogdoches merchants brew their best wassail and let shoppers vote for a winner. This is a simple version to make at home. There’s no alcohol in it.

You’ll need

  • 2 quarts apple cider
  • 2 cups orange juice
  • 1 cup pineapple juice
  • ½ cup lemon juice
  • ½ cup sugar or honey, to taste
  • 3 cinnamon sticks
  • 1 teaspoon whole cloves
  • 1 teaspoon whole allspice
  • 1 orange, sliced

How to

  1. Put everything in a big pot or slow cooker.
  2. Bring to a bare simmer, then turn the heat to low. Don’t let it boil hard.
  3. Keep it warm for at least 30 minutes so the spices steep. Strain out the whole spices before serving, or ladle around them.

Source for the tradition: Visit Nacogdoches, Wassail Fest and Country Roads Magazine.
