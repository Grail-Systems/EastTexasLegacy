What one big straw does to our water.

Every well pulls the water down around it in a funnel, called a cone of depression. Pick how much a new user would pump, then watch the funnel spread through the Carrizo-Wilcox, year by year. The numbers come from the state’s own study of Angelina and Nacogdoches counties.

How it works

In 1970, the state’s consultants figured out how far wells would pull down the Carrizo in Angelina and Nacogdoches counties. They used a standard formula hydrologists have used since the 1930s, called the Theis equation. They treated the outcrop to the north as a line where rain refills the sand. This page runs that same math.

Their own example used a well pumping 500 gallons a minute, a transmissivity of 10,000 gallons a day per foot, a storage coefficient of 0.00005, and an outcrop 15 miles away. We start with those values, except transmissivity. We use 20,000, the middle of the range the state measured for the Carrizo here.

Source: TWDB Report 110, “Coefficients of Transmissibility, Permeability, and Storage” and “Interference Between Wells and Long-Term Drawdowns,” pp. 27–34. Storage coefficients of 0.00007 to 0.0002 for the Carrizo: TWDB Report 327 (1991), p. 13. Power plant request: Powered Land, from Pineywoods GCD records.

What this can’t tell you

  • It’s one straw in an empty glass. It shows only the new well. Every other well already pumping adds its own funnel on top. The state wrote that these funnels “are additive.”
  • It’s not the state’s full groundwater model. The Texas Water Development Board runs a much bigger computer model of the whole aquifer. That’s our next step. Until then, treat these numbers as a fair estimate, not a forecast.
  • The sand isn’t the same everywhere. The state measured a wide range. Try the low and high ends under “Change the aquifer numbers.”
  • It doesn’t know where any project would put its wells. No data center here has filed for a well permit, and Amp Z’s water use is still listed as “TBD.”
