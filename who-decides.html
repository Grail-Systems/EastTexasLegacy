Power and land

Who decides the route?

Every big change in this county started with a few people deciding where something would go: a railroad, a town, a mill, a highway. Here’s how those calls got made, from the county’s own history.

1882

The railroad picks the town

Lufkin wasn’t settled and then reached by the railroad. The railroad came first. The Houston, East & West Texas line reached Denman Springs in October 1882, and town lots were sold that September on credit at 8% interest.

Within ten years, people were pushing to move the county seat from Homer to the new rail town. The county history notes that one argument was getting the land records close to the railroad, where land agents could get at them easily. Lufkin became the county seat in 1892.

Sources: Bob Bowman, ed., Land of the Little Angel: A History of Angelina County, Texas (Lufkin: Lufkin Printing Co., 1976).
1900

A town sold at auction

Huntington began the same way. Two promoters, E. A. Blount of Nacogdoches and W. J. Townsend of Lufkin, filed a plat in June 1900 and auctioned the lots on June 22 to the highest bidders. They named the town for C. P. Huntington, who ran the Southern Pacific. The rail line, not the people, set the spot.

Sources: Bob Bowman, ed., Land of the Little Angel: A History of Angelina County, Texas (Lufkin: Lufkin Printing Co., 1976).
1934–1940

The mill, the money and the newspaper

Southland Paper Mills, the first plant to make newsprint from Southern pine, was built east of Lufkin on what had been a cotton field. Ground was broken Jan. 14, 1939, and the first roll came off the machine Jan. 17, 1940.

The county history says the backers pledged nearly 110,000 acres of timberland and half of a railroad as security, then asked a federal agency, the Reconstruction Finance Corporation, for a loan of about $3.4 million. Public credit helped build a private mill.

In 1934, while the Lufkin Daily News was promoting that mill, a group led by J. H. Kurth, W. C. Trout and the Hendersons bought the paper. The people with the most to gain owned the newspaper telling the story.

Sources: Bob Bowman, ed., Land of the Little Angel: A History of Angelina County, Texas (Lufkin: Lufkin Printing Co., 1976); Texas Historical Commission marker, Southland Paper Mills (Hwy 103 east of Lufkin).
1973

A million acres, owned from New York

By 1973, Temple Industries had merged with Time Inc. The county history counts more than a million acres of East Texas timberland under that combined company. Decisions about our woods and water were now being made by a New York media company.

Sources: Bob Bowman, ed., Land of the Little Angel: A History of Angelina County, Texas (Lufkin: Lufkin Printing Co., 1976).
1956–1966

The tree the public saved, until it didn’t

When the new courthouse went up in the 1950s, people fought to keep a big old tree on the square, and the county history says the public won. About ten years later the tree was cut down anyway, without much notice. A public win only lasts as long as somebody keeps watching.

Sources: Bob Bowman, ed., Land of the Little Angel: A History of Angelina County, Texas (Lufkin: Lufkin Printing Co., 1976).
Now

Same question, new projects

The old Southland mill site at Herty is the ground now proposed for a data center. Different century, same question: who decides, and does the public get a say before the route is set? That’s why we file records requests and show up to meetings.
